An embedding model converts a piece of text into a fixed-length vector of
numbers such that texts with similar meaning end up close together in the
resulting vector space. Modern embedding models are themselves neural
networks, typically transformer encoders trained on large amounts of text
with a contrastive objective that pulls semantically similar pairs of
texts closer together and pushes dissimilar pairs apart.

The choice of embedding model matters a great deal for retrieval quality.
General-purpose models trained on broad web text work reasonably well
across domains, but a model fine-tuned on domain-specific text, such as
legal documents or medical literature, usually retrieves more relevant
passages within that domain. Embedding dimensionality is also a practical
trade-off: higher-dimensional embeddings can capture more nuance but cost
more to store and search over.

A subtle but important failure mode is embedding drift. If a corpus is
re-embedded with a different or updated embedding model while old vectors
from a previous model are left in the index, the two sets of vectors are
not comparable to each other, and similarity search across them becomes
unreliable. In production RAG systems, an embedding model upgrade
therefore requires re-embedding the entire corpus, not just new documents.
