Retrieval-Augmented Generation, commonly abbreviated RAG, is a technique for
grounding a language model's output in an external knowledge source at
inference time. Instead of relying only on facts memorized during training,
a RAG system fetches relevant text passages from a document store and
inserts them into the model's prompt before generation. This lets the model
answer questions about information it was never trained on, such as a
company's internal policies, a paper published last week, or a codebase
that changes daily.

The core idea splits language modeling into two separable jobs. Retrieval
handles finding the right information. Generation handles turning that
information into a fluent, well-formed answer. Because the two jobs are
separate, each can be improved independently: a better retriever improves
answer accuracy without retraining the language model at all, and a bigger
or newer language model improves fluency without touching the knowledge
base.

RAG became popular because pure parametric generation, where a large
language model answers purely from what it memorized during training, has
two structural weaknesses. First, the model's knowledge is frozen at its
training cutoff, so it cannot know about anything that happened afterward.
Second, when a model does not know an answer, it tends to produce a
fluent, confident-sounding guess rather than admitting uncertainty, a
failure mode commonly called hallucination. Grounding generation in
retrieved, verifiable text directly addresses both problems.
